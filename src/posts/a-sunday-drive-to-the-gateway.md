We set off at around 09:15 on 30 August 2026. The weather was unusually pleasant for Mumbai: a bright morning broken by patches of soft grey cloud drifting in from the Arabian Sea, taking some of the edge off the heat. Sunday mornings are supposed to be among the city's calmer hours, although Mumbai roads tend to interpret the word *calm* rather loosely.

The plan was simple enough. We would head south through the city, take the Bandra-Worli Sea Link and the Coastal Road, and eventually end up at the Gateway of India. It had been years since I had last visited that part of Colaba, and with the roads relatively clear, it felt like a good excuse to take the Jeep Compass out for a proper morning run.

## NH48 and the usual surprises

Once on NH48, I settled into the flow of traffic and headed south. It did not take long before the first strange moment of the morning appeared. On the left, a Maruti Dzire was reversing along the highway shoulder, apparently after missing wherever its driver had intended to go. There were no hazard lights and no obvious warning, just a car travelling in precisely the direction you do not expect a car on a highway to be travelling.

The incident lasted only a few seconds as we passed, but moments like that immediately reset your attention. Defensive driving in Mumbai is less about assuming everyone else will follow the rules and more about leaving enough margin for the possibility that somebody nearby is about to do something completely unpredictable.

The rest of the run toward Bandra was comparatively uneventful, and before long the road opened out toward the Bandra-Worli Sea Link. That transition is always satisfying. The dense cityscape suddenly falls away, the road rises above the water, and for a few kilometres Mumbai feels unusually open. With the skyline in the distance and the Arabian Sea stretching out on either side, the character of the drive changes almost immediately.

## The joy of the machine

This is also the sort of road where the Jeep Compass makes a lot of sense. The steering has a reassuring amount of weight to it, and at highway speeds the car feels settled and planted. On the longer open stretches of the Sea Link, I could simply set the cruise control and let the car maintain a steady pace while the city and water moved past on either side.

What made the stretch enjoyable was not speed so much as the absence of constant interruption. For once there were fewer scooters cutting across lanes, fewer sudden braking points and fewer gaps to judge every few seconds. It gave me enough space to simply appreciate the way the car behaved on an open road rather than constantly reacting to everything around it.

From Worli, we continued onto the Coastal Road. The newer sections create a strange contrast with much of the rest of Mumbai. One moment you are surrounded by buildings and traffic, and the next you are on wide, smooth roads beside the sea before dropping into the tunnel beneath the city.

Inside the tunnel, I switched on the speed limiter and settled just below the posted 60 km/h limit. The smooth asphalt, the steady ventilation noise and the repeating amber lighting give the tunnel an almost mechanical rhythm, making it one of the few places in Mumbai where driving briefly feels predictable.

That predictability did not last very long. A Kia Seltos came past through the dedicated bus lane, moving considerably faster than the posted limit before disappearing ahead. Between that and the reversing Dzire earlier in the morning, the roads had already provided their share of questionable decisions for what was supposed to be a quiet Sunday drive.

## South through Mumbai

Coming out of the Coastal Road and continuing toward South Mumbai, the surroundings began to change again. The newer roads gradually gave way to older streets, stone buildings and the denser urban fabric around Fort and Colaba. It is one of the things I like about driving across Mumbai: travelling twenty or thirty kilometres can feel less like crossing a single city and more like passing through several different versions of it.

By the time we reached Colaba, it was around 10:00. I had not been to the Gateway of India in years, and at that hour on a Sunday morning the plaza was still relatively manageable. The heavier tourist crowds had not fully arrived yet, leaving a mixture of morning walkers, families, photographers and people simply spending some time by the water.

After parking, we walked toward the promenade. The harbour opened out ahead beneath the morning clouds, dotted with ferries near the jetties and cargo vessels farther offshore. Beyond them, the opposite shoreline faded gradually into the haze.

There is a noticeable change in pace when you arrive at the waterfront after an hour of roads, tunnels and traffic. A few minutes earlier, everything around you demands attention: vehicles, lanes, signals, mirrors and the constant movement of the city. At the promenade, most of that disappears into the background and the view opens almost entirely toward the sea.

## The Gateway and the Taj

The Gateway itself was familiar even after all those years away. It is one of those landmarks that has been photographed so many times that it can begin to feel almost abstract until you are standing in front of it again. In person, the scale returns. The stonework, the arches, the water immediately beyond it and the movement around the plaza give the place a presence that photographs never quite reproduce.

Across from it stood the Taj Mahal Palace Hotel, its red dome rising above the surrounding buildings.

It is difficult to look at the hotel without also remembering what happened there during the attacks of 26 November 2008. Even on an otherwise ordinary Sunday morning, that history remains part of the place. At the same time, the area around it was simply moving through another day: taxis arriving and leaving, pedestrians crossing toward the Gateway, hotel staff working near the entrance and tourists stopping to take photographs.

That coexistence of history and ordinary life is something Mumbai does particularly well. Places carry enormous weight, but the city continues moving around them without turning them into monuments frozen in time.

We spent some time walking along the promenade and watching the boats before eventually heading back. There had been nothing particularly complicated about the morning; it was simply a drive across the city, through NH48, the Sea Link and the Coastal Road, ending at Colaba.

What made it memorable was the collection of smaller moments along the way: the absurd sight of a car reversing down a highway shoulder, the open stretch across the Sea Link, the controlled rhythm of the Coastal Road tunnel, and finally the harbour opening up at the end of the route. After spending the morning moving through one of the busiest cities in the country, the drive ended at a place where the road could go no farther and the Arabian Sea simply took over.
