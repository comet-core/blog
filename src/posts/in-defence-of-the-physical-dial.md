You are driving down a narrow highway at ninety kilometres an hour. 

It is pitch-black outside, raining hard enough that the wipers are sweeping rhythmically, and a thin skin of condensation is beginning to creep up the inside of the windscreen. 

In a car with thoughtful ergonomics, you don't even move your eyes. Your left hand drops from the steering wheel toward the console, locating a knurled rotary dial by texture alone. Two clicks clockwise—felt through a firm, dampened detent—and the fan surges to life. A flick of an adjacent rocker directs air toward the glass, and within seconds the condensation starts to clear. Throughout, your eyes never left the wet asphalt or the taillights ahead.

Now imagine doing that in a car designed over the past decade. 

Your hand reaches toward a vast, featureless sheet of glossy black glass. Because glass has no topography, you cannot navigate it by touch. You have to turn your head away from the road and look. 

First, you hunt for the climate icon. But because full-screen navigation is running, temperature controls are minimized into a tray along the bottom bezel. You reach out, the car hits a highway seam, your finger bounces, and you accidentally tap the seat heater instead. Now you are hot, annoyed, and still fogged up. You glance back at the road, look down again, hunt for the fan slider, and drag a thumb across flat glass with zero resistance. 

At ninety kilometres an hour, taking your eyes off the road for three seconds means your two-ton machine has travelled seventy-five metres completely unmonitored. We were told this was the future, but in practice, it is design malpractice.

## The manufacturing equation

How did we arrive at an automotive landscape where even adjusting the cabin temperature or angling the air-conditioning vents requires negotiating an operating system?

Automakers dressed this shift up in the language of high technology. They called it "minimalism," promising serene, Scandinavian cockpits inspired by consumer electronics. But behind the aesthetic pitch lay a pragmatic reality: consolidating controls alters the manufacturing equation.

The manufacturing incentives are real, even if the economics are less straightforward than they appear. A single central display can replace a panel containing dozens of individual switches, reduce the number of separate components to assemble, and allow manufacturers to reuse much of the same hardware across different trim levels. Functions that once required dedicated switchgear can instead be implemented through software.

None of this makes the touchscreen itself free, nor does it mean every physical control is expensive. The attraction is flexibility: the manufacturer can change the interface without redesigning the entire dashboard. But that flexibility comes with a trade-off. A reduction in physical complexity on the production line can mean an increase in cognitive complexity for the person behind the wheel.

The question isn't whether manufacturers save money by consolidating controls. It's whether the savings justify making ordinary driving tasks harder to perform.

## The hand is an eye

The fundamental flaw of the touchscreen cockpit is a misunderstanding of human biology.

The human hand is an exquisite sensory instrument. Packed with mechanoreceptors, fingertips can discern textures down to fractions of a millimetre, judge spring tension, and confirm physical state changes without a single photon of light.

When you flip a physical toggle switch, your nervous system receives three distinct layers of feedback:
* **Spatial orientation**: Your hand knows where the switch is relative to the steering wheel because it occupies a fixed, three-dimensional coordinate in space.
* **Mechanical confirmation**: The switch requires deliberate force to actuate, and it responds with a physical snap that travels straight through the bones of your finger.
* **State memory**: You can tell whether a switch is toggled up or down simply by resting a fingertip on it in the dark.

On a flat touchscreen without meaningful haptic feedback, every control offers much the same tactile information: cold, inert glass. Because touchscreens cannot communicate through touch, they are parasitic. They are forced to steal the only other high-bandwidth sensory channel you have available: your eyes. And in a moving automobile, your eyes already have a full-time job.

This is not merely a preference for old-fashioned hardware. A 2026 driving-simulator study comparing touchscreen and physical controls found greater visual distraction with touchscreens, alongside worse driving performance on tasks requiring multiple interactions. The precise outcome depends on the interface and the task, but the underlying problem is straightforward: when operating a control requires looking at it, part of the driver's visual attention is diverted from the road.

A well-positioned physical control reduces that demand by giving the hand something familiar to locate and operate. It doesn't eliminate distraction, but it can reduce the amount of visual searching involved.

## The machine that pushes back

There is something deeper at stake here than mere ergonomics. It is about the character of the machines we choose to live with.

A car is not a smartphone. A smartphone sits passively in your hand while you lounge on a sofa; it exists in a universe of low consequences. A car is an analog, kinetic beast operating in a world of high inertia, weather, friction, and biological fragility. 

Driving is an engagement with physics: the texture of the tarmac through the steering rack, the chassis settling in a bend, and the bite of brake pads against the rotors. 

When the controls of that machine are physical, the interface feels connected to the reality of the machine itself. A knurled dial has mechanical sympathy. It acknowledges that moving a vehicle through space is a craft that requires tactile focus, muscle memory, and respect for physical forces. Treating the car as a digital appliance—an iPad on wheels that happens to have tyres attached—breaks that covenant.

## The pendulum swings

The good news is that the industry is beginning to face more scrutiny over this trend, and safety assessments are starting to reflect the problem.

In 2026, Euro NCAP introduced revised assessment protocols that examine how drivers interact with essential controls. Indicators, windscreen wipers and washers, hazard lights, the horn, and the eCall emergency function are among the controls assessed for direct, accessible operation. Well-positioned physical controls can score well because they reduce the need to navigate menus while driving.

This isn't a law forcing manufacturers to restore every button, and a touchscreen-heavy car can still earn five stars overall. The change is subtler: the usability of the driver's interface is becoming a more explicit part of the safety conversation. A car can be excellent at protecting its occupants in a crash while still making some routine driving tasks unnecessarily distracting.

That distinction matters. Safety isn't limited to what happens when a collision occurs; it also includes the interface a driver has to use to avoid one.

Technology is supposed to serve human attention, not consume it. Sometimes the most sophisticated interface for a frequently used driving function isn't a high-resolution OLED panel running an elaborate graphical interface. It is a spring, a detent, and a rotary knob that your hand can locate in the dark.
