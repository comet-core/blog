Most space telescopes are patient machines.

Hubble moves with deliberate precision, settling onto a target and collecting light for as long as the observation demands. The James Webb Space Telescope operates far beyond Earth around the second Sun–Earth Lagrange point, staring into the infrared universe with the kind of patience required when the photons you want have been travelling for billions of years.

The **Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory** was built for a panic.

Launched in November 2004, Swift was designed primarily to study **gamma-ray bursts (GRBs)**: brief, extraordinarily energetic flashes produced by some of the most violent events in the universe. Some are associated with massive stars collapsing into black holes; others come from collisions between compact objects such as neutron stars. The most powerful can release, in seconds, an amount of energy comparable to what the Sun will radiate over its entire lifetime.

There is one inconvenient detail for astronomers: gamma-ray bursts have absolutely no interest in appointment schedules. A flash appears without warning somewhere in the sky, blazes for anything from fractions of a second to several minutes, and then begins fading almost immediately. By the time an observatory discovers the event, determines where it happened, sends the coordinates to Earth and waits for someone to approve a new target, much of the interesting part may already be over.

So NASA built Swift like a sprinter.

## The autonomous sprint

Swift’s defining feature is not the size of its telescope. It is how quickly the entire observatory can react.

Its **Burst Alert Telescope (BAT)** continuously watches a large area of the sky for sudden increases in gamma radiation. When BAT detects a likely burst, Swift’s onboard computers calculate its position and begin sending an alert toward Earth within seconds. At the same time, the spacecraft automatically turns itself so its narrower X-ray and ultraviolet/optical telescopes can examine the fading afterglow.

Depending on where the burst appears relative to the spacecraft, Swift can reposition itself in roughly tens of seconds to a little over a minute. There is no committee meeting, no controller manually dragging a target marker across a screen, and no polite request asking the universe to hold the explosion for another five minutes.

**Swift sees something explode and immediately goes after it.**

Once the coordinates reach Earth, automated alerts are distributed to astronomers around the world. Ground-based telescopes can then turn toward the same patch of sky while the event is still evolving. A gamma-ray flash detected by a spacecraft in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) can therefore set off a chain reaction involving observatories scattered across several continents.

It is about as close as astronomy gets to an **emergency dispatch system**.

That rapid-response architecture eventually made Swift useful for much more than the mission it was originally built around. Over the following two decades it observed supernovae, stellar flares, active galaxies, comets and asteroids, and stars being torn apart after wandering too close to supermassive black holes. When gravitational-wave astronomy arrived, Swift could also help search for electromagnetic counterparts to events detected by instruments on Earth.

A telescope designed to chase one class of transient ended up becoming a general-purpose sentinel for a universe that refuses to sit still.

## The man who made it Swift

In 2018, NASA renamed the spacecraft the **Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory**, honouring the astrophysicist who had served as the mission’s founding principal investigator.

Neil Gehrels spent much of his career at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center and was one of the people responsible for turning Swift from an unusual proposal into a working spacecraft. The central idea was ambitious for the time: instead of requiring humans to decide every move, give the observatory enough autonomy to detect an unpredictable event and react while the science was still happening.

That philosophy aged extremely well.

Modern astronomy increasingly depends on finding things that change: explosions, mergers, flares, objects appearing where nothing was visible before. Swift was built around that style of observing years before the phrase **time-domain astronomy** became attached to an entire generation of surveys and instruments.

Its original prime mission was supposed to last two years. Swift has now survived for more than twenty-one.

The instruments were not what eventually became the problem.

The orbit was.

## Running out of sky

Swift circles Earth in low Earth orbit and was launched without a propulsion system capable of periodically raising that orbit. Every pass through the extremely thin upper atmosphere produces a tiny amount of drag, gradually removing orbital energy and lowering the spacecraft.

For most of Swift’s life, that process happened slowly enough to ignore.

Then the Sun became considerably less cooperative.

At Swift’s altitude, that meant encountering more atmospheric particles on every orbit and therefore experiencing more drag. The spacecraft was still scientifically productive, but its altitude began falling faster than expected.

NASA was left with an unusual problem: a healthy observatory whose instruments could continue working, attached to an orbit that could not.

The solution was equally unusual.

In 2025, NASA contracted Katalyst Space Technologies to build a robotic spacecraft called **LINK**. The plan was to rendezvous with Swift, physically capture a satellite that had never been designed for servicing, and slowly raise it into a safer orbit.

If it worked, a spacecraft launched in 2004 without any means of refuelling or boosting itself would effectively receive an orbital life extension more than two decades later.

LINK launched on **3 July 2026** aboard a Pegasus XL rocket, and for a short while the situation looked remarkably hopeful. Engineers now had two spacecraft in orbit, one falling toward the atmosphere and another specifically sent up to catch it.

Then LINK developed serious attitude-control problems.

Failures affecting its reaction wheels and cold-gas thruster system made controlling the servicing spacecraft increasingly difficult. Engineers recovered enough capability to continue testing, but attempting to physically capture Swift under those conditions would have introduced more risk than the rescue was meant to solve.

On **19 August**, NASA and Katalyst abandoned the boost attempt. LINK could still be useful for demonstrating rendezvous and proximity operations, but it would no longer raise Swift’s orbit.

The rescue spacecraft had reached orbit. The observatory was still alive. The two simply could not complete the final handshake.

## Back to work

While NASA waited for LINK, Swift had spent months operating in a special low-drag configuration designed to preserve as much altitude as possible. Its X-ray and ultraviolet/optical telescopes were switched off while engineers tried to stretch the orbit long enough for the servicing mission to arrive.

Once the boost was abandoned, there was little reason to continue saving an orbit that could no longer be restored.

So NASA switched the telescopes back on.

Swift resumed scientific observations on **26 August**, returning to the same work it had performed for more than two decades. Current projections place the spacecraft on course to descend below roughly 300 kilometres relatively soon, after which atmospheric drag increases rapidly and continued operation becomes progressively harder.

That gives Swift an unusual final phase. The spacecraft is not failing internally, nor has it exhausted some critical supply of fuel. It is simply running out of altitude while most of the machine remains perfectly capable of doing science.

And apparently Swift intends to keep doing exactly that for as long as physics allows.

## The last look

There is an almost unfair symmetry to how this mission may end.

For more than twenty years, Swift has spent its life chasing things that are disappearing. A massive star collapses. Two neutron stars collide. A black hole tears apart something that strayed too close. Somewhere in the sky, a source suddenly brightens, and Swift turns toward it before the evidence fades away.

Now the observatory itself has become part of the same category.

Its orbit is shrinking, the attempt to rescue it has failed, and there is no propulsion system aboard capable of reversing the descent. Yet Swift is not sitting dormant waiting for re-entry. Its instruments are operating again, the spacecraft is still responding to targets, and astronomers are using whatever observing time remains.

There is no need to manufacture much tragedy around that. Swift was designed for a two-year mission and instead remained useful for more than two decades, surviving long enough to watch entire branches of astronomy develop around the kind of rapid observations it was built to perform.

Most aging machines gradually become less capable as their end approaches. Swift’s problem is almost the opposite: the telescope can still do its job. The sky is still full of targets. The alerts can still arrive.

It is simply losing the ground beneath its orbit.

For twenty-one years, Swift’s entire existence has revolved around one question: *can we turn toward it before the light is gone?*

**For now, the answer is still yes.**
