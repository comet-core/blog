Most space telescopes are contemplative creatures.

Hubble moves with the measured, cautious grace of a Victorian astronomer adjusting a brass focuser. The James Webb Space Telescope hovers a million miles away at the second Lagrange point, shielded from heat, spending hours aligning its golden hexagonal mirrors before quietly soaking in the faint infrared glow of the first galaxies. They are built for patience.

The Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory was built for a panic.

Launched in November 2004, Swift was designed to solve one of the most violent puzzles in modern astrophysics: Gamma-Ray Bursts (GRBs). These are the catastrophic death throes of massive collapsing stars or the collisions of ultra-dense neutron stars billions of light-years away. They release more energy in a few seconds than our Sun will emit over its entire ten-billion-year lifetime.

The problem has always been that GRBs are fleeting. A flash appears in an unannounced corner of the sky, blazes with terrifying brilliance for a fraction of a second or a few minutes, and then rapidly fades into the dark. If you spend an hour adjusting your gyroscopes and waiting for ground controllers in Maryland to approve the command, the show is already over.

So NASA built Swift like a sprinter.

## The autonomous sprint

Swift’s superpower is its agility.

Sitting aboard the spacecraft is the Burst Alert Telescope (BAT), a wide-field instrument constantly watching a huge swath of the sky for sudden, anomalous spikes of high-energy gamma rays. The moment the BAT detects a candidate burst, it doesn’t wait for human permission.

Within fractions of a second, the spacecraft’s onboard computers calculate the exact coordinates and fire up its reaction wheels. The entire 1.5-tonne observatory slews across the sky at up to 50 degrees per minute, swinging its narrower, more powerful X-ray and ultraviolet/optical telescopes directly onto the dying flash within 20 to 75 seconds.

It is the closest thing astronomy has to an emergency dispatcher.

As soon as it locks on, Swift automatically blasts the coordinates down to Earth via the Tracking and Data Relay Satellite System (TDRSS). Within seconds, automated alerts ping observatories on every continent, prompting radio dishes in the Australian outback and giant mountain-top mirrors in Chile to pivot toward the exact same patch of sky.

For more than two decades, Swift has been the sentinel that caught the universe in the act.

## The man who made it swift

In 2018, NASA officially renamed the mission the **Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory** to honour its founding Principal Investigator. 

Neil Gehrels was a quiet, relentless force at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center. He championed the audacious idea that a space telescope should be allowed to make its own decisions in real-time, long before autonomous navigation became fashionable. Gehrels led the mission from its inception until his passing in 2017, fostering an international collaboration that fundamentally changed how we understand black hole formation, tidal disruption events, and the transient sky.

Under his leadership, Swift didn't just study gamma-ray bursts. It discovered supermassive black holes tearing wandering stars apart, mapped comets swinging past Earth, and provided crucial optical follow-ups when gravitational wave detectors first felt two neutron stars colliding.

It was supposed to last two years. It has been running for over twenty.

## Running out of sky

Yet Swift has a quiet vulnerability built into its design: it was launched into Low Earth Orbit (~600 kilometres up) without any onboard propulsion system. There are no thruster tanks, no hydrazine reserves, and no rocket engines to raise its altitude.

For years, that was manageable. But over the last couple of years, Solar Cycle 25 reached its peak activity. Solar storms and coronal mass ejections dumped energy into Earth’s upper atmosphere, heating the thin thermosphere and causing it to expand outward like a rising loaf of bread.

Satellites in low Earth orbit suddenly found themselves plowing through a much thicker soup of atmospheric molecules.

For Swift, that extra drag has accelerated its orbital decay. Without thrusters to push back against gravity, its altitude is slipping downward year by year. Current estimates suggest that within the next few years, Earth's atmosphere will claim the observatory entirely in an uncontrolled reentry, burning up what remains of its instruments over the ocean.

NASA teams and international partners have investigated creative possibilities—such as robotic docking or private orbital tugs—to boost the telescope and buy it another decade of life. But as it stands today, Swift is slowly, inevitably running out of sky.

## The last look

There is a striking poetry to how Swift is spending its remaining time.

Most aging machines slow down as the end approaches. Swift is doing the exact opposite. Orbiting Earth roughly fifteen times a day, it continues to sprint across the heavens with the same frantic urgency it had on day one.

It is a machine that spent two decades watching the sudden, violent extinction of stars that died when the universe was young. And even as the quiet pull of Earth's atmosphere tightens its grip, the sentinel is still turning, still searching, and still catching the dying light before it disappears.
