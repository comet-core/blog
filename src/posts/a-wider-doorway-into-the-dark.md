I had the NASA livestream open from 3:50 PM IST on 30 August 2026.

There is a quiet rhythm to the hour before a launch. The voices over the communications loop are measured and steady, almost conversational, as teams work through propellant loading, telemetry, weather and final checks while viewers around the world watch a rocket standing silently beneath the Florida morning sky.

At 4:56 PM IST — 7:26 AM local time at Kennedy Space Center — the countdown reached zero.

The engines ignited, Falcon Heavy began to rise, and over the broadcast came the call that will probably remain tied to this mission for years:

> **“Go Roman! And go Falcon Heavy!”**

Launch Complex 39A disappeared behind an enormous cloud of exhaust and steam. Falcon Heavy cleared the tower, carving a brilliant streak across the blue, and for a few minutes everything else felt paused.

On 30 August 2026, NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope began its journey outward.

The launch proceeded almost exactly to plan. Thirty-one minutes after liftoff, Roman separated cleanly from Falcon Heavy's upper stage and began flying on its own, already communicating with controllers on Earth.

## A wider way of seeing

We have grown used to deep space being seen through a narrow keyhole.

Hubble transformed astronomy by showing us the universe with extraordinary sharpness. Webb pushed even deeper into the infrared, revealing galaxies, stars and planetary systems that had previously been hidden from us.

Roman was built to complement them by doing something different.

![NASA Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope payload fairing on Falcon Heavy]({{ '/assets/images/roman_fairing.jpg' | url }})

*The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope payload fairing atop Falcon Heavy prior to rollout. (Photo: SpaceX)*

Its primary mirror is 2.4 metres across — roughly the same diameter as Hubble's — but Roman can observe a vastly larger area of sky at once. Its Wide Field Instrument is a 300-megapixel infrared camera with a field of view at least a hundred times larger than Hubble's, while still preserving similarly sharp detail.

That combination is what makes Roman unusual.

Hubble is extraordinarily good at studying small regions of sky in exquisite detail. Roman is designed to take that kind of sharpness and apply it across enormous surveys.

Where Hubble might study an individual tree, Roman is designed to begin mapping the forest around it.

Roman carries two main scientific instruments.

The **Wide Field Instrument**, or WFI, will carry out the huge surveys for which the telescope was designed, studying billions of galaxies, stars and planetary systems.

The **Coronagraph Instrument** is more experimental. It will demonstrate advanced techniques for blocking the overwhelming glare of nearby stars so that much fainter planets and surrounding disks can be observed directly. It is primarily a technology demonstration, but if it performs well, it could help pave the way for future telescopes designed to image Earth-like worlds around other stars.

The telescope carries the name of Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first Chief of Astronomy and its first female executive.

She entered astronomy at a time when women were routinely discouraged from careers in science, yet became one of the central figures behind NASA's development of space-based astronomy. Roman spent years bringing scientists and engineers together and building support for an ambitious space telescope that would eventually become Hubble.

Her role earned her the nickname **“Mother of Hubble.”**

There is something fitting about the telescope that now carries her name.

Roman is not merely intended to look farther.

It is designed to look **widely**.

## Mapping the invisible universe

One of Roman's biggest goals is to understand parts of the universe we cannot see directly.

Astronomers know that the expansion of the universe is accelerating, but the cause — which we call **dark energy** — remains poorly understood. Roman will repeatedly measure enormous numbers of galaxies and supernovae, allowing astronomers to reconstruct how the universe has expanded over billions of years.

It will also study how galaxies are distributed and how their light is subtly distorted by gravity, helping researchers map the otherwise invisible distribution of **dark matter**.

Rather than observing dark matter or dark energy directly, Roman will measure the effects they leave behind.

The sheer scale of its surveys is crucial. Tiny effects that are difficult to detect in individual objects become measurable when repeated across millions or billions of galaxies.

Roman will effectively turn large portions of the universe into a statistical laboratory.

## Hunting worlds that barely reveal themselves

Roman's other major strength lies much closer to home: the Milky Way.

It will conduct an enormous census of exoplanets using a technique called **gravitational microlensing**.

When one star passes almost perfectly in front of another from our point of view, the gravity of the foreground star bends and magnifies the background star's light. If the foreground star has a planet, the planet's own gravity can briefly alter that magnification.

For a short time, an otherwise invisible planetary system announces that it exists.

Microlensing is particularly powerful because it can detect planets at orbital distances that are difficult for many other methods to probe.

It can also reveal something even stranger.

Some planets may not orbit stars at all.

They may have been thrown out of their original planetary systems and now travel through interstellar space alone. Because these **rogue planets** produce almost no light of their own, they are extraordinarily difficult to detect.

Roman's microlensing survey could discover hundreds of them.

For the first time, astronomers may begin to understand just how common these orphaned worlds really are.

## A million miles to L2

Roman is now beginning a roughly three-month journey toward the second Sun-Earth Lagrange point, or L2, approximately one million miles — 1.5 million kilometres — from Earth.

It will not sit motionless at L2. Instead, Roman will enter a large orbit around the region.

This location provides a stable observing environment because Earth and the Sun remain in roughly the same direction from the spacecraft's perspective. That makes it easier to shield the telescope from heat and sunlight while keeping its instruments thermally stable.

Roman's commissioning began almost immediately after launch.

Its six-panel Solar Array Sun Shield deployed successfully just one hour and twenty-three minutes after liftoff, providing electrical power while also helping protect the observatory from solar heating.

Over the following days, controllers will deploy its high-gain antenna and aperture cover, perform trajectory corrections and gradually activate its systems.

Several weeks into the journey, Roman's Wide Field Instrument will be powered on. Its infrared detectors will operate at roughly 90 kelvin, cold enough to reduce thermal noise while observing faint infrared light arriving from deep space.

Reaching L2 will not mean Roman is immediately ready for science.

The telescope still has to complete an extensive commissioning process. Instruments must be activated, optics aligned, detectors calibrated and pointing systems tested. Engineers will spend months ensuring that a spacecraft assembled on Earth behaves exactly as intended in an environment more than a million kilometres away.

If everything continues as planned, Roman's first images are expected in early 2027.

Then the surveys begin.

## The things nobody planned for

Roman was designed around specific questions.

Dark energy.

Dark matter.

Exoplanets.

The structure and evolution of the universe.

But some of the most important discoveries made by astronomical observatories are often things nobody originally built them to find.

A telescope capable of repeatedly surveying enormous regions of the sky will notice changes.

Stars will brighten.

Galaxies will flare.

Objects will move.

Events that last only days, hours or perhaps even minutes may appear inside datasets originally collected for completely different reasons.

Roman will produce an enormous archive of the changing infrared sky.

And somewhere inside it may be something nobody currently knows to search for.

That may ultimately become one of the telescope's most important qualities.

> *We are a fragile, terrestrial species that builds delicate arrangements of glass, metal and silicon, straps them to controlled explosions, and sends them millions of kilometres from home simply because we want to know what is out there.*

Watching from my desk half a world away, I saw the telemetry numbers climb and the curvature of Earth appear behind the vehicle.

Then Roman separated from Falcon Heavy's upper stage and drifted slowly away.

The launch was over.

Thirty-one minutes earlier it had been sitting on a launch pad in Florida.

Now it was an independent spacecraft, communicating across space and heading toward a destination a million miles from Earth.

There is still a long commissioning period ahead. Thousands of components have to behave correctly. Instruments have to wake up. Calibration has to be completed. The observatory has to reach L2 and prove that everything built and tested on Earth works just as well in deep space.

Only then will Roman begin doing what it was built for.

Not simply staring deeper into one tiny patch of darkness, but opening our view across enormous pieces of the universe at once.

Our feet remain firmly planted on Earth.

And somewhere beyond them, a new eye has begun its journey toward the dark.
