I had the NASA livestream open from 3:50 PM IST on 30 August 2026.

There is a quiet rhythm to the hour before a launch. The voices over the communications loop are measured and steady, almost conversational, as teams work through propellant loading, telemetry, weather, and final checks while viewers around the world watch a rocket standing silently beneath the Florida morning sky.

At 4:56 PM IST — 7:26 AM local time at Kennedy Space Center — the countdown reached zero.

The engines ignited, Falcon Heavy began to rise, and over the broadcast came the call that will probably remain tied to this mission for years:

> “Go Roman Space Telescope! And go Falcon Heavy!”

Launch Complex 39A disappeared behind an enormous cloud of exhaust and steam. Falcon Heavy cleared the tower, carving a brilliant streak across the blue, and for a few minutes everything else felt paused.

On 30 August 2026, NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope began its journey outward.

The launch had gone almost exactly to plan. Thirty-one minutes after liftoff, Roman separated cleanly from Falcon Heavy's upper stage and began flying on its own, already communicating with controllers on Earth.

## A wider way of seeing

We have grown used to deep space being seen through a narrow keyhole.

Hubble transformed astronomy by showing us the universe with extraordinary sharpness. Webb pushed even deeper into the infrared, revealing galaxies, stars, and planetary systems that had previously been hidden from us.

Roman was built to complement them by doing something different.

![NASA Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope payload fairing on Falcon Heavy]({{ '/assets/images/roman_fairing.jpg' | url }})

*The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope payload fairing atop Falcon Heavy prior to rollout. (Photo: SpaceX)*

Its Wide Field Instrument is a 300-megapixel infrared camera capable of maintaining extremely sharp resolution across enormous areas of sky. Roman's field of view is at least a hundred times larger than Hubble's, allowing it to survey the universe at a scale that previous flagship observatories simply were not designed for. NASA expects it to survey the sky roughly a thousand times faster than Hubble.

Where Hubble might study an individual tree in extraordinary detail, Roman is designed to begin mapping the forest around it.

The telescope carries the name of Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first Chief of Astronomy and its first female executive. She entered astronomy at a time when women were routinely discouraged from careers in science, yet became one of the central figures behind NASA's development of space-based astronomy.

Roman spent years bringing scientists and engineers together, building support within NASA and government for an ambitious space telescope that would eventually become Hubble. Her role earned her the nickname **“Mother of Hubble.”**

There is something fitting about the telescope that now carries her name.

Roman is not merely intended to look farther. It is designed to look **widely**.

Its surveys will map billions of galaxies and study how cosmic structure and the expansion of the universe have evolved, giving astronomers new ways to investigate dark matter and dark energy. It will conduct an enormous census of planetary systems throughout the Milky Way, including planets detected through gravitational microlensing that may otherwise be almost impossible to find.

Some of those worlds may not orbit stars at all.

They may simply wander through interstellar space.

## A million miles to L2

Roman is now beginning a roughly three-month journey toward the second Sun-Earth Lagrange point, or L2, approximately one million miles — 1.5 million kilometres — from Earth.

It will not sit motionless at L2. Instead, Roman will enter a large orbit around the region, where the combined gravitational effects of Earth and the Sun, together with the spacecraft's orbital motion, provide a stable environment for astronomical observations.

Roman's commissioning began almost immediately after launch.

Its six-panel Solar Array Sun Shield deployed successfully just one hour and twenty-three minutes after liftoff, providing electrical power while also helping thermally shield the observatory. Over the following days, controllers will deploy its high-gain antenna and aperture cover, perform trajectory corrections, and begin activating its instruments.

Several weeks into the journey, Roman's Wide Field Instrument will be powered on. Its infrared detectors will be kept at about 90 kelvin, allowing them to detect faint infrared photons while the telescope begins the long process of calibration and commissioning.

If everything continues as planned, Roman's first images are expected in early 2027.

And after that comes the part nobody can completely predict.

Roman was designed around specific questions — dark energy, dark matter, exoplanets, and the structure of the universe — but a telescope capable of repeatedly surveying enormous areas of sky will inevitably see things its designers were not specifically searching for.

That may ultimately become one of its most important qualities.

> *We are a fragile, terrestrial species that builds delicate arrangements of glass, metal and silicon, straps them to controlled explosions, and sends them millions of kilometres from home simply because we want to know what is out there.*

Watching from my desk half a world away, I saw the telemetry numbers climb and the curvature of Earth appear behind the vehicle. Then Roman separated from Falcon Heavy's upper stage and drifted slowly away.

The launch was over.

Thirty-one minutes earlier it had been sitting on a pad in Florida. Now it was an independent spacecraft, communicating across space and heading toward a destination a million miles from Earth.

There is still a long commissioning period ahead. Instruments have to activate, optics have to settle, calibrations have to be completed, and thousands of individual systems have to work exactly as intended before Roman can begin doing the science it was built for.

But the hardest boundary had already been crossed.

Our feet remained firmly planted on Earth.

And somewhere beyond it, a new eye had begun its journey toward the dark.
